\subsection{The ladder}

The instrument is a short ordered list of assertions, each of which can be
checked on a given machine without asking the original authors anything. In
increasing order of demand:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A citable published record for the work exists.
\item The reference source can be obtained at a fixed, named revision.
\item The runtime that the reference source requires is available on the machine.
\item The published table can be recomputed on the machine.
\end{enumerate}

Three properties make this more useful than a pass-or-fail verdict. It is
\emph{monotone}: a break at one rung makes every rung above it unreachable, so
the informative quantity is the index of the first break and not a count of
failures. It is \emph{machine-relative}: the same work can reach rung two on one
machine and rung four on another, and the ladder records which machine and when
rather than pretending to a universal property. And it is \emph{refutable}: each
rung names the observation that would settle it, so a reader with a different
machine can disagree with a specific line rather than with a conclusion.

Two things are stipulated not to satisfy a rung, because both are common and
both quietly convert a break into a false pass. A different interpreter that
executes a similar language is not the required runtime; source written for one
numerical environment may run, fail, or silently produce different results under
another, and treating the substitute as equivalent is an assumption, not a
reproduction. And a table produced by a re-implementation is not the published
table, however carefully the re-implementation was written.

\subsection{What monotonicity buys, and what it costs}

Monotonicity is stated above as a property of this particular list. It is worth
separating the property from the list, because everything the instrument offers
follows from it and nothing follows from the specific four assertions we chose.

\begin{definition}[Ladder]\label{def:ladder}
Fix a set $\mathcal{S}$ of machine states, where a state is a machine at an
instant. A \emph{ladder} of length $K$ is a sequence of predicates
$r_1,\dots,r_K$ on $\mathcal{S}$ satisfying the \emph{demand condition}
\[
  r_{k+1}(s) = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; r_k(s) = 1
  \qquad\text{for every } s \in \mathcal{S},\ 1 \le k < K .
\]
The \emph{observation} at $s$ is the vector $r(s) = (r_1(s),\dots,r_K(s))$, and
the \emph{first break} is
$\rho(s) = \min\{k : r_k(s) = 0\}$, with $\rho(s) = K+1$ when no rung fails.
\end{definition}

The demand condition is a constraint on what may be written down as a rung,
rather than an empirical finding about scientific software, and an assertion
that fails it is disqualified rather than accommodated.

\begin{proposition}[The index is the whole observation]\label{prop:sufficient}
Under Definition~\ref{def:ladder}, every realized observation is one of the
$K+1$ non-increasing vectors in $\{0,1\}^K$, so the image $r(\mathcal{S})$
contains at most $K+1$ values. Let $I=\rho(\mathcal{S})\subseteq
\{1,\dots,K+1\}$. The map $\bar\rho:r(\mathcal{S})\to I$ defined by
$\bar\rho(r(s))=\rho(s)$ is a bijection. Consequently $r(s)$ is recoverable
from $\rho(s)$, and an audit that reports the integer discards nothing it
observed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The demand condition forces $r(s)$ to be non-increasing in $k$, so each realized
vector is a run of ones followed by a run of zeros. There are $K+1$ possible
such vectors, but a fixed state space need not realize all of them. For every
realized vector, the position of its first zero (or $K+1$ when there is none)
uniquely identifies the vector; hence $\bar\rho$ is well-defined and bijective
onto its realized index set $I$.
\end{proof}

This is the difference between a ladder and a checklist, and it is the whole
practical case for the instrument. A badge scheme records an unordered set of
attributes and a reader must hold all of them at once; a ladder records an
ordinal, so audits of different artifacts on different machines can be compared,
ranked and tabulated as numbers.

\begin{proposition}[Refinement preserves the order]\label{prop:refine}
Let $L'$ be obtained from a ladder $L$ by inserting further predicates so that
the demand condition still holds and every rung of $L$ survives in its original
relative position. Then for any two states $s, t$,
\[
  \rho_L(s) < \rho_L(t) \;\Longrightarrow\; \rho_{L'}(s) < \rho_{L'}(t).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $k = \rho_L(s)$ and let $k'$ be the position of that rung in $L'$. Then
$r'_{k'}(s) = 0$, so $\rho_{L'}(s) \le k'$. Since $\rho_L(t) > k$, every rung of
$L$ up to and including $k$ holds at $t$; the rung at $k'$ therefore holds at
$t$, so $\rho_{L'}(t) > k' \ge \rho_{L'}(s)$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:refine} is what makes the instrument usable by more than
one person. A four-rung audit and a seven-rung audit of the same two artifacts
cannot be read off each other, but they cannot contradict each other about which
artifact got further, provided the longer ladder refines the shorter one. A
community can therefore lengthen the ladder without invalidating what has
already been recorded against it.

The cost is exactly as sharp. An assertion that is worth recording but is not
comparable to the others cannot be inserted at all.

\begin{proposition}[An incomparable assertion is not a rung]\label{prop:offladder}
Let $a$ be a predicate on $\mathcal{S}$ that is incomparable to some rung $r_k$,
in the sense that both $a \wedge \neg r_k$ and $r_k \wedge \neg a$ are satisfiable.
Then no position in the sequence makes $L \cup \{a\}$ satisfy the demand
condition.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Placing $a$ below $r_k$ violates the condition at the state satisfying
$r_k \wedge \neg a$; placing it above violates it at the state satisfying
$a \wedge \neg r_k$. Both such states exist by hypothesis, so every possible
insertion position violates one of the adjacent implications required by the
demand condition.
\end{proof}

Corpus availability is such an assertion, which is why this study keeps it off
the ladder rather than choosing a position for it. A corpus can be perfectly
public, openly licensed and permanently archived while absent from the machine,
and it can be present on a machine that has none of the runtime the source
needs; both directions of Proposition~\ref{prop:offladder} are realised in
practice. Calling that state either reachable or broken loses the distinction
that matters, so we record the public status and the local status as two
separate facts and report them beside the index rather than inside it.

\subsection{The probe}

The project being audited already carried a recorded status for each of these
questions. That record is not what this study reports, because a recorded status
ages: a runtime can be installed, a corpus can be fetched, a pinned revision can
be moved. Instead a read-only probe re-checks each rung against the live machine
and writes what it observed with a timestamp, and the manuscript records the probe
output with its checksum.

The probe resolves the required interpreter and the common substitute on the
executable search path; confirms that the reference source directory exists,
reads its revision identifier, compares it against the recorded pin, and counts
how many of the recorded entry points are present; and checks a list of candidate
locations for the evaluation corpus, including the location named by the
project's own data-root environment variable. It installs nothing, downloads
nothing, and executes no vendor code. Its output records the first broken rung
directly, so the headline of the audit is a number the probe computed rather than
a reading of prose.

Reporting a probe of this kind is itself a design choice worth stating: a probe
can be re-run where a recollection cannot, and only an audit that can be re-run
can be contradicted.

\subsection{The comparison that does remain reproducible}

The same project contains a fully local simulation study built on an open-source
room-acoustics package~\cite{scheibler2018pyroomacoustics}, and it supplies the
second half of this paper. Sources are localised from time-of-arrival
measurements at a microphone array in which each channel carries an unknown
constant timing offset. Two estimators are compared. The offset-agnostic
estimator solves an ordinary least-squares problem with all offsets fixed at
zero. The offset-aware estimator treats the offsets as unknown parameters with a
prior and returns a maximum a posteriori estimate over source position and
offsets jointly.

The independent variable is the dispersion of the true per-channel offsets across
\NConditions{} conditions, from a condition in which the offsets are exactly zero
up to a condition in which they are large relative to the measurement noise. Each
condition draws \NTrials{} trials, and both estimators are run on the same trial,
so the comparison is paired within trial. The reported quantity is the median
localisation error. That choice is inherited from the project's own display
rule rather than made here, and it matters: the error distribution has a heavy
upper tail, so the mean is pulled by outliers. Means are recorded in the
underlying artifacts and are not deleted, but they are not promoted to the
display quantity.

Each condition is tested with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank
test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} from a standard scientific computing
library~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}, paired per trial. Alongside the test we report
the count of trials on which the offset-aware estimator was the better of the
two, because a count of \NTrials{} paired outcomes distinguishes a small
consistent advantage from a large but mixed one in a way a $p$ value does not.
The simulation was run once at a recorded seed and is not re-run here; this study
reads its output rather than regenerating it.

\subsection{A third comparison, recorded twice over}

The same project holds one further record, and it is unusual in a way this study
exploits. It compares two locally written estimators for the same calibration
problem across \CxConditions{} levels of timing noise at
\CxTrialsPerCondition{} trials each, and it stores both a cell summary of the
kind a paper prints and the individual paired trials that summary was computed
from. Having both in one file is what makes them readable against each other;
everywhere else in this project, and in most published work, one of the two is
absent and a reader cannot tell whether the printed row and the underlying
trials would name the same winner.

The two estimators differ in which timing measurements they are given. One is
fitted to both the microphone-to-source and the source-to-source time
differences; the other to the source-to-source differences alone. Both are local
Gauss--Newton implementations written inside this project rather than anyone's
released code, and this study names them by what they are given rather than by
whose method they resemble, because the comparison here is between ways of
summarising a record and not between published methods. The findings below are
about the summaries of these two local implementations and leave every
published estimator as its authors published it; the paper claims no novelty in
acoustic localisation.

The record declares its own display rule in a field: the median error is
primary, the mean is labelled secondary and annotated as outlier-sensitive. That
declaration is inherited, not made here, and the analysis below checks that it
still says so before reading anything else. We compare three ways of reducing
one condition's trials to a statement about which estimator was better: the mean
over trials, the median over trials, and the per-trial paired comparison, tested
with the same two-sided signed-rank procedure used for the offset sweep. Every
quantity is a re-summary of the stored pairs; the simulation is not re-run, and
each cell summary the record carries is re-derived from the trials it summarises
before any of it is reported, so a file assembled from two different runs stops
the build.

Two trials of the \CxTrialsPerCondition{} at two of the conditions produced no
error at all: one estimator diverged and its errors are written into the record
as the bare token \texttt{NaN}, which the interchange format has no way to
express. A strict reader rejects the file and a permissive one accepts a
number-shaped thing that is not a number. We treat those values as absent rather
than as large, count them, and check that the count of absent values equals the
number of failed runs times the number of quantities a run reports.

\subsection{Why a reproduced table is not a checked claim}

The ladder stops at recomputing the published table. That is the strongest rung
we could state, and what a reader wants lies one step beyond it.

Let $D$ be the per-trial record of a paired comparison, let $T$ be a summary
such as the mean or the median, and let $c$ map a summary to the conclusion
drawn from it, here the name of the better estimator. What a paper prints is
$T(D)$ and what it concludes is $c(T(D))$.

\begin{proposition}[A summary determines only its own conclusion]\label{prop:disclosure}
Fix summaries $T$ and $T'$. The conclusion $c(T'(D))$ is recoverable from
$T(D)$ for every record $D$ only if $c \circ T'$ factors through $T$. For $T$
the mean and $T'$ the median this fails: there are records $D, D'$ with
$T(D) = T(D')$ and $c(T'(D)) \ne c(T'(D'))$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The second claim is a construction. Take two paired records with the same two
arm means, and move mass within one arm from many trials to one, which leaves
the mean fixed and moves the median across the other arm's median.
\end{proof}

The construction is elementary. What matters is that the situation it describes
occurs in the record described above, at a condition where nothing is missing
and no handling decision has been made, and this study reports it as an
observation rather than as a possibility.

Proposition~\ref{prop:disclosure} says that reaching the top rung certifies the
reproduction of a number and not of a claim, and that the gap between the two is
closed only by disclosure of $D$ itself. We therefore record disclosure as a
second off-ladder axis, beside corpus availability: it fails
Proposition~\ref{prop:offladder} for the same reason, being satisfiable by work
that cannot be run and unsatisfied by work that can.

One further consequence concerns the trials that produced nothing. When one arm
returns fewer usable trials than the other, an average over what remains is
conditioned on that arm not having failed.

\begin{corollary}[Complete-case averaging favours the arm that fails]\label{cor:completecase}
Suppose failures in an arm are associated with larger error, in the sense that
the error of a run conditioned on completing is stochastically smaller than the
error of a run conditioned on nothing. Then the complete-case mean of that arm
is smaller in expectation than its unconditional mean, so a comparison of
complete-case means is biased towards whichever arm failed more often.
\end{corollary}

The direction matters for reading what follows. At the two conditions where a
run was lost, the loss was in one arm, and dropping it can only have helped that
arm; the conclusion the paired test reaches there is therefore reached against
the drop rather than because of it.

\subsection{A condition the design does not have}

Disclosure closes the gap between a printed summary and the trials underneath
it. It cannot close a second gap, which is between the trials that exist and the
conditions nobody ran. This subsection states that gap and describes the sweep
we ran to measure it, which is the one experiment in this paper performed for
this manuscript rather than read out of an existing record.

\begin{definition}[Break-even of a paired comparison]\label{def:breakeven}
Let a paired comparison of two estimators be indexed by a nuisance parameter
$\sigma \ge 0$, and let $m_A(\sigma)$ and $m_B(\sigma)$ be a summary of each
arm's error, with $d(\sigma) = m_A(\sigma) - m_B(\sigma)$ so that $d < 0$ names
arm $A$. Suppose $d(0) < 0$. The \emph{break-even} is
\[
  \sigma^\ast = \inf\{\, u > 0 : d(\sigma) > 0 \text{ for all } \sigma \ge u \,\},
\]
with $\sigma^\ast = \infty$ when no such $u$ exists. On a finite grid
$G \subset [0,\infty)$ the break-even is \emph{located} if $d$ changes sign
between two adjacent points of $G$, and \emph{censored above} $\max G$ if
$d(\max G) \le 0$.
\end{definition}

Two features of the definition are doing work. It is stated for whatever summary
$m$ a study uses, so a comparison has one break-even per summary rule rather than
one break-even; and censoring is a distinct outcome from a large value, because
a sweep that stops before the sign changes has not measured where it changes.

The estimand in the refined experiment is consequently finite-grid and
room-indexed. For room $r$ and observed grid
$G=\{\sigma_0,\ldots,\sigma_J\}$, we first observe
\[
  \widehat d_{r,G}(\sigma_j)
  = m_{A,r}(\sigma_j)-m_{B,r}(\sigma_j),
  \qquad j=0,\ldots,J,
\]
and report a located crossing only when the observed signs bracket a change.
The reported interval is the pair of adjacent grid values (with the
within-cell interpolation shown in the figure); it is not an estimate of a
continuous root without an additional smoothness model. A room whose final
observed sign has not changed contributes a right-censored value
$\sigma^\ast_r>\max G$. The range across rooms is therefore a description of
the crossed design, not a population parameter for all rooms.

\begin{proposition}[A grid, not a pair of estimators, fixes the reported sign]
\label{prop:gridsign}
Let $g = \min (G \setminus \{0\})$ and suppose the first observed point is
strictly beyond the break-even, $g > \sigma^\ast$. Then $d(\sigma) > 0$ at
every non-zero point of $G$, and there exist comparisons $d$ and $\tilde d$
agreeing at every point of $G$ with $\sigma^\ast > 0$ for one and
$\sigma^\ast = 0$ for the other. No function of the cells at $G$ distinguishes
them.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first claim is Definition~\ref{def:breakeven}. For the second, choose
$0<\epsilon<g$ and construct two functions that agree on $G$: set
$d_0(0)=d_\epsilon(0)=-1$, set $d_0(\sigma)=1$ for every $\sigma>0$, and set
$d_\epsilon(\sigma)=-1$ for $0<\sigma<\epsilon$ and
$d_\epsilon(\sigma)=1$ for $\sigma\geq\epsilon$. Their break-evens are,
respectively, $0$ and $\epsilon$, while every non-zero grid point has value
$1$. Thus the cells at $G$ are identical although the break-even differs; any
statistic computed from those cells takes the same value on both. If a
continuous curve is required, the transition inside $(0,g)$ can be smoothed
without changing any grid value.
\end{proof}

The consequence is the one this paper cares about. A sweep reports a sign, the
sign is a joint property of the estimators and the grid, and the grid is chosen
by the same authors who report the sign --- ordinarily for good reasons, since a
grid is placed where the modelled effect is expected to matter. Nothing in the
cells warns a reader that the placement decided the answer.

\begin{corollary}[One condition can falsify it]\label{cor:onecondition}
If $\sigma^\ast > 0$ and there exists an unobserved condition
$\sigma_0 \in (0,\sigma^\ast)$ with $d(\sigma_0)<0$, measuring that condition
makes the negative sign observable; no quantity of additional trials at points
of $G$ does. Under an additional one-crossing assumption, any point in that
interval has this property, but no such shape assumption is needed here.
\end{corollary}

This separates the two off-ladder axes cleanly. Disclosure of the per-trial
record is recoverable after the fact, because the trials were run and can be
released; a condition that was never run has no trials to disclose, and the only
repair is a new experiment. That is why the sweep described next exists.

To measure $\sigma^\ast$ we reran the offset comparison on a grid refined inside
the interval the original design skipped, from zero up to its lowest non-zero
condition, at \FineLevels{} dispersions and \FineTrials{} paired trials each.
Both estimators are the original implementations, imported without modification;
the prior width, the assumed measurement noise and the trial count are the values
the original run fixed. What differs is the design. The refined grid is crossed
with \FineRooms{} microphone arrays, one of them the array the original sweep
used and the rest independently simulated at recorded seeds, so that a
break-even can be reported as a range across rooms rather than as a single number
from one. Each trial is seeded from a hash of its own cell --- design, array,
dispersion, trial index --- so that a cell does not depend on the rest of the
grid or on the order in which the grid was walked. \IndependenceCells{} cells
were recomputed afterwards out of order, and again after a condition absent from
the grid had been run, and reproduced bit for bit; that is the property
Corollary~\ref{cor:onecondition} needs in practice, since a reader adding a
condition should not have to rerun the ones already recorded. Every trial of
every cell is stored, which is the disclosure this paper argues for applied to
its own new record.

One quantity was fixed before the sweep ran and is recorded in its output: the
break-even predicted by interpolating the original sweep's own printed medians
between its two lowest conditions, together with the tolerance
(\PredictionTolerance{} ms) within which we agreed in advance to call the
prediction successful. The prediction uses nothing the original record does not
already print.

The distinction between the two disclosures is also formal here. The cell
summary is a map from the paired record to one number per arm, whereas the
per-trial disclosure retains the paired vector itself. A reader can apply a new
summary or a paired test only to the latter; no interpolation or re-analysis can
recover a condition that is absent from $G$.

\subsection{Evidence discipline}

No number in this manuscript is typed by hand. Every artifact it rests on,
including the probe output, is recorded with its SHA-256 checksum in a manifest
that accompanies the manuscript source. The figure and table code verifies each
checksum against the bytes on disk before reading them and aborts on any mismatch,
and it emits both the figures and the numeric macros that the text prints. Even
the corpus size quoted above is extracted from the recorded project inventory rather
than retyped, so the manuscript cannot disagree with the record it cites.
