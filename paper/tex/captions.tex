\newcommand{\CapLadder}{%
Reachability of a published comparison, as observed on one machine on
\ProbeDate{} by a read-only probe rather than read off a previously recorded
status. Each rung is an assertion that can be checked independently, and the
rungs are ordered by how much they demand. Green marks a rung that holds here and
red marks one that does not. The ladder is monotone, so the first break makes
every rung above it unreachable and the informative quantity is the index of that
break rather than a count of failures. The grey strip records a fact that is
deliberately not a rung: the evaluation corpus is public, openly downloadable and
permanently archived, and is nonetheless absent from this machine, which is a
different situation from either a satisfied or a broken rung.%
}

\newcommand{\CapSweep}{%
Median localisation error against the dispersion of the unknown per-channel
timing offsets, for an offset-agnostic least-squares estimator and an
offset-aware maximum a posteriori estimator, over \NTrials{} paired trials at
each of \NConditions{} conditions. The vertical axis is logarithmic because the
two estimators differ by more than two orders of magnitude at one condition. In
the shaded condition the true offsets are exactly zero, and the ordering between
the estimators reverses: the offset-agnostic estimator recovers the source almost
exactly, while the offset-aware estimator pays a floor cost for estimating
nuisance parameters that are not present. At every condition where offsets do
exist, the offset-aware estimator is the better of the two and its error is
nearly flat, while the offset-agnostic error grows with the dispersion it
ignores.%
}

\newcommand{\CapWins}{%
The same comparison counted per trial rather than summarised by a median. Bars
give the number of the \NTrials{} paired trials at each condition on which the
offset-aware estimator produced the smaller error, and the dashed line marks the
count expected if the two estimators were indistinguishable. Where offsets are
present the offset-aware estimator wins a clear majority but never all trials,
so the advantage is consistent rather than absolute. Where offsets are absent it
wins none at all, which is a stronger and more specific statement than a small
$p$ value: the reversal is unanimous across trials, not an average over a mixed
outcome.%
}

\newcommand{\CapDisplayRule}{%
One set of trials, three summaries, and the answers they give. A third
comparison in the same project puts two locally written estimators for the same
calibration problem against each other at \CxConditions{} levels of timing
noise, \CxTrialsPerCondition{} paired trials each; one estimator is fitted to
both available sets of time-difference measurements and the other to one of
them. Each cell names the estimator that the summary in its row calls the better
of the two, with the quantities that decide it. The three rows are not three
experiments: they are three ways of reducing the same trials to a sentence. The
top row is outlined where it names a different estimator from the two below it,
which happens at \CxDisagreeing{} of the \CxConditions{} conditions, including
one at which every planned trial returned an error in both arms so that no
handling of failures is involved.%
}

\newcommand{\CapPairedTrials}{%
The individual paired trials that the summaries above were computed from, which
is the disclosure this study argues for, drawn once so that its value can be
seen. Each point is one trial at one timing-noise level, positioned by the error
each estimator produced on it; the diagonal is a tie, and points below it are
trials won by the estimator given both measurement sets. The axes are
logarithmic and share a range across the three panels. The cloud sits below the
diagonal at every level while a small number of points sit two orders of
magnitude above it, and that combination is the entire disagreement between the
mean and the median. A short mark on the left axis is a trial on which one
estimator diverged and returned no error at all, drawn at the error the other
estimator did return, because such a trial has no position in the plane and
placing it at an edge would invent a value for it.%
}

\newcommand{\CapInfluence}{%
How much of a printed conclusion one trial in \CxTrialsPerCondition{} is
carrying. The solid curve gives the gap between the two estimators' mean errors
after setting aside each estimator's own largest trials, from none set aside up
to five; the dashed curve is the same gap between medians at the same scale.
Below zero a summary names the estimator given both measurement sets. At the
levels where the mean disagrees with the median it crosses zero after a single
trial is set aside, marked with a ring, so the conclusion a reader would draw
from the mean is decided by one trial out of \CxTrialsPerCondition{} while the
median moves hardly at all. Setting trials aside is a measurement of leverage
here and not a proposal: no quantity reported as a result in this paper is
computed from a trimmed sample.%
}

\newcommand{\CapBreakeven}{%
What a refined grid finds in the interval a coarse one skipped. The comparison
between an offset-agnostic and an offset-aware localisation estimator was rerun
on a grid of \FineLevels{} offset dispersions crossed with \FineRooms{}
independently simulated microphone arrays, \FineTrials{} paired trials in each
cell, using the same estimator code as the coarse sweep and differing from it
only in which conditions were run. Left: the difference between the two
estimators' median errors, one line per array; below zero the offset-agnostic
estimator is the better of the two. The upper row of ticks marks the conditions
the coarse sweep places in this range, of which there are two, both at the ends;
the lower row marks the \FineLevels{} conditions of the refined sweep. Right:
the dispersion at which each array's ordering changes sign, located under two
summaries of the same trials, with the dashed line at the position predicted
beforehand from the coarse sweep's own printed numbers. Arrays whose ordering
has not changed sign by the top of the refined grid are drawn as arrows leaving
it, because a sweep that stops below a crossing has not measured one and
recording its last condition as the answer would invent a measurement.%
}

\newcommand{\CapEfficiency}{%
The same two estimators measured against the same theoretical bound, summarised
two ways. The bound is the Cram\'er--Rao limit computed from the array geometry
and the timing noise at each trial, so it is a property of the problem rather
than of either estimator and it is identical under both summaries; all of the
spread here comes from the numerator. Filled markers divide the mean error by
the bound and hollow markers divide the median error by the same bound. Read
through the mean, both estimators appear between \CxEffMeanLow{} and
\CxEffMeanHigh{} times worse than the best any unbiased estimator could do, and
which of them looks closer changes with the noise level. Read through the
median, both sit between \CxEffMedianLow{} and \CxEffMedianHigh{} times the
bound throughout. A median below the bound is not a contradiction, because the
bound constrains the root mean square error and not the median.%
}
